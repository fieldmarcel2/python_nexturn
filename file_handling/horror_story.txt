THE DROWNING HOUSE

The real estate agent's smile faltered when I asked about the water stains on the basement ceiling. "Just old damage," she said too quickly. "The house has been empty for three months. Nothing to worry about."

I should have listened to that instinct—the one that screamed at me to walk away. But the price was too good, the Victorian mansion too beautiful, and I was desperate to escape my cramped apartment downtown. So I signed the papers without asking enough questions.

The inspection report mentioned "previous water intrusion" and "foundation settling," but nothing alarming. The real estate agent assured me the previous owners had simply abandoned the house after inheriting it from an elderly relative. They never even tried to live here. That should have been my warning.

I moved in on a Tuesday in October.

The house smelled wrong from the beginning—not the musty odor of an empty property, but something fouler. Something organic and ancient. I opened every window despite the chill, burning candles and incense. By the second night, the smell had faded enough that I could almost ignore it.

The first incident happened on my fourth day.

I woke at 3 AM to the sound of water dripping. Not a steady patter, but an irregular, almost breathing rhythm—drip... drip-drip... drip. I traced it to the guest bedroom, where a dark spot had appeared on the ceiling. But it wasn't raining. I checked outside. The sky was clear and starlit.

I went back to bed, telling myself it was a pipe. Old houses had pipes. Pipes leaked. This was normal.

By the end of the first week, the dripping had spread to three rooms. Water appeared on ceilings where no pipes ran. When I called a plumber, he spent two hours investigating and found nothing. "No active leaks," he said, genuinely puzzled. "And the roof is sound. This is... strange."

The water left stains—not brown or yellow like typical water damage, but a peculiar grey-blue, almost like diluted ink. When I touched it, the stain felt warm and slightly tacky, like something had just dried. When I smelled it close, the awful odor returned in full force.

I bought a dehumidifier and tried not to think about it.

The dreams started in the second week.

In them, I was drowning. Not in water, but in something thicker, denser. I couldn't breathe, couldn't fight it. The pressure crushed down on my chest, and I saw a face—bloated and pale, with empty eyes that seemed to look directly at me. The face opened its mouth, and I woke gagging.

Every night, the same dream. Every night, that face.

I researched the house's history online. The listing had been up for nearly two years before I bought it. But the property records went back further—to 1987, when the original owner, a Dr. Marcus Webb, had purchased it. I found an article in the local newspaper archives, dated June 1994.

"Local Man Drowns in Own Home—Cause Unknown"

Dr. Marcus Webb, 54, was found dead in the basement of his Victorian mansion, having apparently drowned despite being in a sealed room with no water sources. Medical examiners were baffled. There was no water in his lungs, yet his body showed all the signs of drowning—the bloating, the skin sloughing, the dark pooling under the eyes. "It's as if he drowned in air," one quoted expert said. "Which, of course, is impossible."

The house had been empty for years before being inherited by distant relatives who never occupied it. Those relatives eventually died or sold, passing the property through several hands. No one ever stayed long.

I sold my car for the realtor's commission, but I couldn't leave. It was irrational—I should have fled that day—but something had changed in me. The dreams had become less frightening and more familiar, almost intimate. When I looked in the mirror, my eyes seemed to retain the bluish tint of water for longer than they should.

The water in the house grew worse.

It started appearing in the shower even when I'd already drained it, pooling in the kitchen sink, collecting on the hardwood floors in places where there was no logical source. It didn't smell like normal water. It smelled like the ocean—brine and decay and something ancient and hungry.

I stopped being afraid. Instead, I felt an strange calling from the basement.

On the twenty-third day, I went down.

The basement was flooded, though impossible—the windows were barred, the doors sealed. But there it was, a pool of that grey-blue water, perfectly still, reflecting the light like dark mercury. And floating in the center of that pool was a door. An ordinary wooden door, standing upright in the water, leading nowhere.

I don't remember stepping into the water. I remember the cold—not the cold of regular water, but a cold that went beyond temperature, a cold that seemed to freeze something essential in my chest. I remember the water tasting of copper and sand. And I remember the face beneath the surface.

Dr. Marcus Webb, or what remained of him. He was suspended in the water, eyes open, mouth moving, as if trying to speak. His hands reached toward me, skin bloated and grey, fingers impossibly long and jointed like a spider's legs.

I tried to scream, but water filled my lungs.

When I woke, I was standing in the hallway, soaking wet. It was dawn. I was still breathing, though my lungs burned like I'd been underwater for hours.

I don't remember deciding to stay. But I couldn't leave. The water had become part of me—I could feel it moving through my veins, filling the hollow spaces of my bones. When I looked in the mirror, I saw the beginnings of that transformation, that bloating at my jaw, that peculiar grey-blue tint creeping into the whites of my eyes.

The water whispers now, every time I approach the basement. It calls my name in a voice like my own, like dozens of voices layered atop each other. It promises to make me part of something vast and patient, something that has been drowning slowly in this house for thirty years.

Last night, I called the realtor.

"I'm withdrawing from the sale," I said. "I'm leaving the house to you."

There was a long pause on the other end.

"But you already own it," she said. "I received your deed transfer three weeks ago."

I hung up.

Now I wait in the basement, watching that door. I can feel the water rising—not rising around me, but rising inside me. Soon, I think, I'll understand why the water moves the way it does. Soon, I'll become part of the rhythm that the house breathes.

Soon, when someone else buys this place, they'll hear the dripping too.

And they'll come to understand that the water doesn't want to leave.

The water never leaves.

It just waits.
